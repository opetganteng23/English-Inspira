Passage: Honeybees and the shape of a hive

For centuries, naturalists admired the hexagonal cells of a honeybee comb without agreeing on how bees built them so precisely. Some argued that bees carry an innate sense of geometry; others suggested that the bees do far less than they appear to.

Recent experiments support the second view. Worker bees begin by shaping warm wax into rough cylinders. As the wax is heated by the bees' own bodies, it softens and flows, and surface tension pulls neighboring cylinders into six-sided walls, much as soap bubbles settle into a tight cluster. In other words, the hexagon is less a blueprint than a by-product of simple physical forces.

This finding does not make the bees' work trivial. Bees must still regulate temperature, choose where to start, and cooperate across thousands of cells. But it does suggest that elaborate order can arise from a few simple behaviors, a lesson that engineers now borrow when designing lightweight materials.
